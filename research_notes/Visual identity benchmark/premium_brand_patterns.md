## Q1. Site by site: what do these brands do for palette, type, layout, navigation, rhythm, CTAs, numbers, motion and voice?

### Takeaway
The best-documented examples share a few traits. The page "chrome" (header, menus, backgrounds) is mostly one or two colours. Square corners are standard. A serif carries emotion while a plain sans or serif does the working text. The signature colour is held back, not spread everywhere. Typefaces are often proprietary (Pictet's Lardy, Cartier's Brilliant Cut and Fancy Cut). Hermès and Aesop show the extreme of restraint. Hermès keeps its signature orange out of the interface entirely. Aesop runs on two colours, parchment and charcoal, with zero corner rounding. Detailed metrics (pixel sizes, letter-spacing, grid columns) could not be checked for any site. That is the main gap.

### Inferences
- **Pattern: brand colour as signature, not as wallpaper.** Hermès keeps its orange out of the UI. Cartier puts its red in packaging and accents on a near-black and white base. Pictet ties its claret to the lion and wordmark. For oxblood #7a2229, this suggests a one-colour header bar and select full-width bands, with most reading surfaces staying warm paper #f2eeea. Oxblood should not tint text blocks, borders and buttons all at once.
- **Pattern: two-voice type.** Pictet uses Lardy Serif plus Sans; Aesop uses Zapf Humanist (emotive) plus Suisse (functional); Cartier uses Fancy Cut serif plus Brilliant Cut sans. "Minimal in type" in this tier means one expressive serif for headlines and one quiet face for working text. Brands commission or license distinctive faces. A Cormorant-character serif for headlines plus a restrained text face matches the pattern. The brief bans "sans-serif everywhere", not sans for small functional labels.
- **Pattern: zero radius.** Aesop and Cartier both run 0px corners. This supports the brief's ban on rounded cards.
- **Pattern: warm off-white, not pure white.** Aesop's #fffef2 parchment is close in spirit to #f2eeea.
- **Pattern: heritage as content, not ornament.** Pictet's lion page, Hoare's history page and Coutts' founders' letters all turn the firm's history into content. A Thai firm can do the same with real founding dates, licences and partner signatures, without adding decoration.
- **Motion:** the only documented motion language is Aesop's "calm transitions". This is consistent with a short rise or fade only.

- No verified data on **exact type sizes, letter-spacing, case, grid columns, header heights, CTA labels or button styling** for any site, because direct inspection was blocked. Next step: open each site with a browser tool and read computed styles.
- Typefaces are unconfirmed for Lombard Odier, Julius Baer (current), Coutts body text, Rothschild & Co, Slaughter and May, Wachtell, Cravath, Lazard, C. Hoare, Loro Piana (web) and Patek (proprietary).

- No boutique accounting firm or family office with a documented, design-press-covered website was found. The search for Centerview, PJT and Evercore returned only business profiles.
- UNVERIFIED (model recall): Wachtell's site (wlrk.com) is a famously sparse, text-led page with almost no imagery. Lazard's identity uses a dark green-black wordmark. Lombard Odier's site leans black and white with large photography. Patek's site has a near-black "dark" presentation. Check all of these in a browser.

## Q2. Which brands use deep red, burgundy, oxblood or claret as a signature, and how do they deploy it without looking loud?

### Takeaway
Among private banks, **Pictet** is the clearest confirmed red-family signature: claret-brick #A04044, tied to its red lion and Lardy wordmark. **Cartier** is the luxury benchmark: a deep-red leather box with gold garland in packaging, and a brighter red used sparingly as an accent on a near-black and white site. **Loro Piana** uses a terracotta-brown "kummel red" and burgundy within a neutral, fibre-coloured world. Lombard Odier, Coutts, Julius Baer and Rothschild & Co are blue-based, or blue plus gold. Berenberg's heritage colour is orange. The sources found give no confirmation that Coutts or Lombard Odier use burgundy.

### Cited Findings
- Pictet primary colour "Apple Blossom" #A04044 with white and black (auto-extracted) — [Brandfetch: Pictet](https://brandfetch.com/pictet.com). Lion emblem with four incarnations; logo evolved in 2021 with Lardy — [Pictet logo page](https://www.pictet.com/us/en/about/the-pictet-logo)
- Cartier red box: official packaging by the 1930s, "deep red leather, gold garland decoration and silk-lined interior". The red-and-gold pairing became so distinctive it "often eclipsed the Cartier logo in recognition" — [Lyon & Turnbull](https://www.lyonandturnbull.com/stories/the-cartier-red-box)
- Cartier web: red #d50032 as one token among near-black, white, near-white and mid-grey; 0px radius — [shadcn.io: Cartier](https://www.shadcn.io/design/cartier)
- Loro Piana: "kummel red (a terracotta brown)" as distinctive colour, inside a quiet-luxury palette that includes burgundy — [Selvane](https://www.selvane.co/blogs/knowledge/the-color-intelligence-of-loro-piana)
- Lombard Odier auto-extracted palette is blues plus near-black; no burgundy found — [Brandfetch: Lombard Odier](https://brandfetch.com/lombardodier.com)
- Rothschild & Co: dark blue #162A4B plus gold #CC9F53 — [Brandfetch](https://brandfetch.com/rothschildandco.com)
- Julius Baer: Royal Blue / Blue Zodiac #141E55 — [FutureBrand](https://www.futurebrand.com/our-work/juliusbaer); [Brandfetch](https://brandfetch.com/juliusbaer.com)
- Berenberg: orange, retained in the 2025 rebrand "with reference to its founding history" — [Berenberg](https://www.berenberg.de/en/news/pressemeldung/berenberg-new-brand-identity/)
- A search for burgundy or claret signatures at Arbuthnot Latham, Weatherbys and C. Hoare returned nothing — [search results incl. Arbuthnot Latham, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbuthnot_Latham)

### Inferences
- **How red stays quiet in these examples:**
  1. It sits on a mostly neutral field: black and white at Pictet and Cartier, natural fibres at Loro Piana.
  2. It is attached to one heraldic or iconic object: Pictet's lion, Cartier's box.
  3. It pairs with gold or brass detail: Cartier's gold garland, the closest analogue to the brief's "brass for figures".
  4. It is a dark, desaturated red (Pictet #A04044; Cartier's box tone ~#801B2B). Bright pure red appears only as a small UI accent (Cartier #d50032).

  The brief's oxblood #7a2229 is darker than Pictet's red and close to the Cartier box tone, so it sits in the "quiet" register.
- **Differentiation:** in a category dominated by navy, plus navy and gold at Rothschild and Julius Baer, an oxblood signature is distinctive. Pictet is the one major private-bank precedent showing it can read as old-money rather than loud. Checking Pictet's live site is the highest-value follow-up.
- **Red plus brass:** Rothschild's gold, Cartier's garland and the brief's brass suggest metal tones work as a *detail* colour (figures, rules, emblems). They become cliché when used as large text or gradients on dark (see Q3).

- Not verified whether any bank or law firm uses a full-width **red header bar**. No source found.
- UNVERIFIED (model recall): the Coutts logo has historically appeared in dark navy with a crown device, and Rothschild & Co's arrows-and-crown symbol appears in gold. Both need checking.

## Q3. What makes these sites read "expensive and trustworthy" rather than "template", and what are the common failure modes?

### Takeaway
"Expensive" in the sources comes from **ownership and restraint**:
- proprietary or distinctive type
- one signature colour used sparingly
- square geometry
- warm paper rather than white
- real heritage material
- object or people photography in place of stock

"Template" comes from the industry default: navy, generic sans, skyline or handshake stock photos, and "delivering results" taglines. Increasingly, a second default has appeared as well: **dark cinematic hero plus gold serif**, now sold as a ready-made wealth-management template.

### Cited Findings
- "Open ten asset manager websites in a row and you will see navy blue, a sans-serif font, a stock photo of a city skyline, and a generic tagline about 'delivering results'" — [Wolf Financial: visual identity guide](https://wolf.financial/blog/visual-identity-design-guide-financial-brands) (attribution via search summary; it may come from a sibling result such as [Chatmeter](https://www.chatmeter.com/resource/blog/branding-for-financial-services/))
- The financial-sector "predictable playbook": "navy blue palettes, conservative serif wordmarks and reassuring imagery of handshakes or ascending charts" — [The Brand Identity: 20(SOMETHING) editorial finance branding](https://the-brandidentity.com/project/20something-uses-editorial-design-to-reinvent-finance-branding)
- "Generic stock photos of handshakes and skylines fail to differentiate the firm" — [Emulent: wealth management website playbook](https://emulent.com/?p=88449) (via search summary)
- Marketplace templates for "Luxe Wealth Management" already ship "dark cinematic imagery, gold serif accents… dramatic full-bleed heroes featuring gold-accented serif typography layered over luxury lifestyle photography" — [Converge template](https://enter.converge.ai/marketplace/templates/luxe-wealth-management-firm-website-629)
- Professional wealth-management sites typically cost $15k–$50k+ (vendor claim) — [Select Advisors Institute](https://www.selectadvisorsinstitute.com/our-perspective/wealth-management-website-design-sai)
- Ownership signals at the top tier: Pictet commissioned Lardy; Cartier has Brilliant Cut and Fancy Cut; Aesop's layouts derive from its packaging — [Pictet](https://www.pictet.com/us/en/about/the-pictet-logo); [shadcn.io: Cartier](https://www.shadcn.io/design/cartier); [Work & Co](https://work.co/clients/aesop/)
- Restraint signals: Loro Piana "communicates expense privately rather than demanding broad public recognition" — [Umbrex](https://umbrex.com/resources/fashion-brand-profiles/loro-piana/); Hermès keeps its signature colour out of the UI — [shadcn.io: Hermès](https://www.shadcn.io/design/hermes)
- Counter-example to the brief's illustration ban: Hermès' January 2026 site uses hand-drawn illustration as a deliberate statement of craft "in the age of AI" — [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91471305/hermes-hand-illustrated-website-is-the-ultimate-luxury); [Domus](https://www.domusweb.it/en/news/2026/01/07/herms-new-website.html)
- Coutts deliberately warmed a stiff category with handwritten type and family-album framing, and FutureBrand reports perception gains — [FutureBrand](https://www.futurebrand.com/our-work/coutts)

### Inferences
- **Failure modes to design against:**
  1. Navy plus sans plus skyline stock (the category default).
  2. **Gold serif on black hero.** This "luxury template" is now itself generic, which matters for the night mode. Brass should stay on figures and rules, never become a gilded headline over dark photography.
  3. Spreading the signature colour everywhere instead of anchoring it.
  4. Rounded cards and soft shadows, which the 0px-radius benchmarks (Aesop, Cartier) avoid.
  5. Free "luxury" display serifs set too large or too light with no supporting text face.
  6. Generic outcome claims ("delivering results") with no specific evidence.
- **"Client-outcome voice" done well** probably means concrete, specific statements in a calm register, the opposite of the "delivering results" cliché. This is supported only indirectly by the sources above.
- The Hermès illustration example is a craft exception: commissioned art from a named artist, rotated seasonally. It does not justify icon grids. The brief's ban still stands.

### Gaps
- No published study or survey quantifying which visual cues raise trust for professional-services sites. The cited claims are practitioner opinion.
- No concrete before-and-after case of a finance firm's premium redesign failing.

## Q4. How do these brands handle dark or night modes, if at all?

### Takeaway
No evidence was found that any of the named private banks, law firms or luxury houses offers a user-selectable dark mode or a `prefers-color-scheme` theme. Some, like Patek, achieve a "dark" mood through dark full-bleed photography inside a light or neutral interface rather than through a theme switch. A true night mode would be uncommon in this tier, which is a possible differentiator but has no precedent to copy.

### Cited Findings
- Patek's site uses "full-bleed dark photography" with white display type and a charcoal, black and taupe palette — a dark *presentation*, not a toggle — [shadcn.io: Patek Philippe](https://www.shadcn.io/design/patek-philippe)
- Hermès and Aesop chrome is light: white stage, cream canvas — [shadcn.io: Hermès](https://www.shadcn.io/design/hermes); [shadcn.io: Aesop](https://www.shadcn.io/design/aesop)
- Technical basis: `prefers-color-scheme` detects OS or user-agent light/dark preference — [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@media/prefers-color-scheme). Practitioner guidance recommends three options (light, dark, system) and saving the user's choice — [natebal.com: dark mode best practices 2026](https://natebal.com/best-practices-for-dark-mode/)
- Practitioner claim: "deep jewel tones like… ruby red" suit luxury dark themes (opinion, low authority) — [Vev: dark mode palettes](https://www.vev.design/blog/dark-mode-website-color-palette/)

### Inferences
- For the brief's "near-black red-brown by night", the closest benchmarks are Patek's charcoal and black palette with no extra chromatic accent, and Cartier's near-black #1d1c1c ink. Both suggest a warm near-black ground that is not pure black, with the brand red and brass kept as small accents. A red-washed screen would cut against this.
- Night mode is where the "gold serif on black" template trap is strongest (Q3). Brass should stay limited to figures in tables; headlines should be in warm off-white.

### Gaps
- Not verified by inspection whether any listed site reacts to `prefers-color-scheme`. The search returned only generic dark-mode articles, so the adoption rate in this tier is unknown.
